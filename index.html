Ward pipeline, four stages

Under a second

First, read four KML layers, merge 54 shared buildings and add 97 census wards into 24.

Three seconds

Then compute rates, shares and gaps for every ward and draw twelve figures.

Thirty-two seconds

Next, fit K-Means for K from 2 to 6 and re-test each fit 230 times with new seeds, subsamples and noise.

Instant

Last, write the Power BI tables that feed the dashboard.

Segment widths follow the measured run time of the ward stages (0.6 s, 3.0 s, 32.2 s, under 0.1 s), with a floor so the short stages stay readable. The same command then trains and tunes the three heart-risk models, writes the Power BI tables and exports the models for the browser. 73 automated tests check the counts, the formulas, the classifiers, and that the browser copy of the models gives the same answers as Python.

Every ward, one click

The heart risk predictor reads like a patient chart: five sections, from the patient to the ECG and imaging. As you type, a bedside monitor shows the vitals and runs its ECG at the heart rate you entered. One click runs all three models.

The overview keeps the headline numbers and a ward lookup that opens any ward's facilities and hospitals list. The charts and the facility map live in a five-page Power BI report, embedded in the dashboard.

Three models, one patient

Next to the ward study sits a second module. Three classifiers read twelve routine cardiology measurements, among them age, chest pain type, resting blood pressure, cholesterol and the exercise ECG, and each gives a probability of heart disease.

They were trained on 800 patients and tested on 200 they never saw. The data come from one hospital in India and have no location, so this module is never joined to the wards.
